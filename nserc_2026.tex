\partheading{II. Most significant contributions to research and development}

\textbf{1. Stage-dependent neural representations of face familiarity.}
My MSc research addressed a long-standing question in visual neuroscience: whether
familiar-face processing relies on representations specialized for face identity or on
mechanisms shared with broader visual recognition. I combined source-resolved,
millisecond-scale MEG with task-optimized neural networks used as controlled models of
visual experience, systematically varying what the networks were trained to recognize.
The work revealed a stage-dependent dissociation: familiarity advanced brain--model
alignment in lateral occipital cortex, whereas its principal fusiform effect was stronger
later alignment. Importantly, face-identity training most consistently constrained the
earlier LOC timing effect across architectures, while later fusiform familiarity
representations remained compatible with broader visual-learning objectives. These
findings move beyond a simple face-specific versus domain-general dichotomy and support
a graded, stage-dependent account of functional specialization. I led the analyses,
figures, interpretation, and dissemination, from initial presentations at CCN 2022,
COSYNE 2024, and CCN 2024 to the resulting first-author manuscript.

\vspace{2pt}%
\textbf{2. Open-source M/EEG tools: MNE-Denoise and Jamica.}
Reliable EEG and MEG analyses depend critically on artifact removal, yet advanced
denoising methods are often fragmented across separate implementations and difficult
to integrate into reproducible workflows. I lead the development of MNE-Denoise,
which unifies denoising approaches within an MNE-native framework for electrophysiological data. In parallel, I contribute
to Jamica, a JAX-accelerated implementation of Adaptive Mixture ICA for EEG/MEG.
Together, these projects translate methodological advances into reusable, documented
research software, lowering barriers to robust denoising and supporting reproducible
M/EEG analysis. My work spans implementation, integration, maintenance, and
community-facing development within the broader MNE ecosystem, including presentation
of MNE-Denoise at the 2026 MNE Sprint at Meta Paris.

\vspace{2pt}%
\textbf{3. Defining emerging directions for artificial neural networks and foundation models in MEG.}
As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated with human electrophysiology,
an important challenge is determining not only which models can be applied to MEG, but
how they should be developed and evaluated to produce scientifically useful
representations of brain activity. I contributed substantially to two complementary
works addressing this transition. Our peer-reviewed \textit{Journal of Neural Engineering}
review systematically examined 119 studies using artificial neural networks with MEG,
organizing the field across decoding, brain--model representation, preprocessing, and
related applications while identifying methodological limitations and priorities for
future research. As second author, I contributed across Sections I--III and led the
writing of Section II. I subsequently served as second author on \textit{A Roadmap for
MEG Foundation Models}, with major contributions to Sections 3--5. This perspective
extends the earlier synthesis toward reusable models pretrained on large neural datasets
and identifies critical choices in data representation, model design, evaluation, and
infrastructure needed for MEG foundation models to mature. Together, these contributions
progress from mapping an emerging field to helping define methodological priorities for
its next generation.

\partheading{III. Applicant's statement}

\nsercsubheading{Research experience}
My research training has progressed from telecommunications engineering and signal
processing through machine learning to computational human neuroscience. This trajectory
has given me complementary expertise in quantitative modelling, scientific programming,
and EEG/MEG analysis. In my doctoral work, I integrate behaviour with whole-brain
electrophysiology and computational decision models to study dynamic decision-making.
Before interrogating the neural data from an existing 32-participant MEG dataset, I
reconstructed and audited its acquisition and behavioural pipelines, resolving event-
alignment irregularities, correcting response-confounded trial classification and
hand-specific motor baselines, and re-evaluating analysis assumptions. I then compared
four sequential-sampling accounts using held-out prediction and recovery/identifiability
analyses. This process strengthened my ability to distinguish robust conclusions from
effects that depend on analysis definitions or evidence coordinates, and to design neural
analyses around validated behavioural quantities. Together with training in source
reconstruction, multivariate and time-frequency analysis, low-dimensional neural
trajectories, connectivity and complexity measures, and reproducible software
development, these experiences provide the technical and conceptual foundation for my
proposed research.
