\partheading{II. Most significant contributions to research and development}

\textbf{1. Stage-dependent neural representations of face familiarity.}
My MSc research addressed a long-standing question in visual neuroscience: whether
familiar-face processing relies on representations specialized for face identity or on
mechanisms shared with broader visual recognition. I combined source-resolved,
millisecond-scale magnetoencephalography (MEG) with task-optimized neural networks used as controlled models of
visual experience, systematically varying what the networks were trained to recognize.
The work revealed a stage-dependent dissociation: familiarity advanced brain--model
alignment in lateral occipital cortex (LOC), whereas its principal fusiform effect was stronger
later alignment. Importantly, face-identity training most consistently constrained the
earlier LOC timing effect across architectures, while later fusiform familiarity
representations remained compatible with broader visual-learning objectives. These
findings move beyond a simple face-specific versus domain-general dichotomy and support
a graded, stage-dependent account of functional specialization. I led the analyses,
figures, interpretation, and dissemination, from initial presentations at CCN 2022,
COSYNE 2024, and CCN 2024 to the resulting first-author manuscript.

\vspace{0pt}%
\textbf{2. Open-source M/EEG tools: MNE-Denoise and Jamica.}
Reliable electroencephalography (EEG) and MEG analyses depend critically on artifact removal, yet advanced
denoising methods are often fragmented across separate implementations and difficult
to integrate into reproducible workflows. I lead the development of MNE-Denoise,
which unifies denoising approaches within an MNE-native framework for electrophysiological data. In parallel, I contribute
to Jamica, a JAX-accelerated implementation of Adaptive Mixture ICA for EEG/MEG.
Together, these projects translate methodological advances into reusable, documented
research software, lowering barriers to robust denoising and supporting reproducible
M/EEG analysis.

\vspace{0pt}%
\textbf{3. Artificial neural networks and foundation models for MEG.}
As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated with human electrophysiology,
an important challenge is determining not only which models can be applied to MEG, but
how they should be developed and evaluated to produce scientifically useful
representations of brain activity. Our peer-reviewed \textit{Journal of Neural Engineering}
review systematically examined 119 studies using artificial neural networks with MEG,
organizing the field across decoding, brain--model representation, preprocessing, and
related applications. I helped synthesize this emerging literature, identify methodological
limitations, and formulate priorities for future work. I then extended this
perspective in \textit{A Roadmap for MEG Foundation Models}, contributing to
recommendations on data representation, model design, evaluation, and infrastructure.

\partheading{III. Applicant's statement}

\noindent\textbf{Research experience.} My background in telecommunications engineering, artificial intelligence, and
computational neuroscience has given me a quantitative foundation for studying how
brain activity evolves over time. Since entering neuroscience, my main interest has
been in using computational and electrophysiological tools to answer questions about
the dynamics of human cognition: how information is represented, how these
representations change across processing stages, and how distributed neural activity
supports behaviour. During my PhD, this interest has become focused on dynamic decision-making. I study how
changing sensory evidence is integrated over time and how brain-wide activity evolves
as a decision is formed and translated into action. To address these questions, I combine
behaviour, EEG/MEG, machine learning and computational models to relate trial-by-trial estimates of
evidence, urgency, and commitment to the evolving dynamics of distributed brain activity.
I am particularly interested in whether decision formation is expressed through changes
in population-level neural trajectories, oscillatory coordination, and large-scale
network dynamics, and how these signatures vary across task demands and individuals.
In parallel, developing reusable analysis pipelines and research software has made
reproducibility a standard part of my research practice.
