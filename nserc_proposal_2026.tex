\proposalheading{Brain-wide neural dynamics of human decisions under changing evidence}

\nsercsection{Background and rationale}
Perceptual decisions are often studied while evidence remains stationary within a trial. These paradigms have established influential accumulation-to-bound accounts, but distinct decision-termination mechanisms can produce overlapping behavioural predictions under some conditions \citep{1--3}. When evidence changes after deliberation has begun, those mechanisms can make different predictions about how the evolving decision state should respond. Dynamic decisions therefore expose a fundamental problem: the brain must continuously balance new information against what it has already accumulated, adapt to the increasing cost of waiting, and eventually commit to an action \citep{1}.

Most decision neuroscience has asked which regions or signals represent evidence, choice, or commitment. A complementary dynamical-systems perspective asks how patterns of activity across neural populations evolve together over time. In this view, a cognitive process can be described as a trajectory through a neural state space---a compact representation of how distributed activity changes from moment to moment \citep{4}. This framework has become influential in systems neuroscience, particularly through invasive recordings in animal motor and decision circuits \citep{4--8}. Translating it to humans remains difficult: non-invasive EEG and MEG provide millisecond-scale access to decision formation, but human studies have more often isolated particular evidence-, urgency-, or choice-related signals than characterized brain-wide population trajectories and their coordination \citep{9--12}. Whole-head MEG provides a rare opportunity to bridge these levels. The remaining gap is therefore not whether dynamic decisions have neural correlates, but how distributed human neural states implement the evolving computation: how current and past evidence shape them, how urgency reorganizes them, how they respond when evidence reverses, and whether commitment becomes resistant to new information or can still be revised.

\nsercsection{Preliminary results}
Both datasets that form the basis of this proposal are already collected and are under active analysis in my PhD. The primary dataset contains 275-sensor MEG with individual MRI from 32 healthy adults performing the Tokens task (16,324 trials). Current MEG analyses reveal a reproducible low-dimensional organization of decision-related oscillatory activity: alpha- and beta-band trajectories evolve systematically with changing evidence, the developing choice, and movement preparation, with contributions from visual and sensorimotor sources \citep{12}. These analyses replicate the core trajectory findings of the original study and are being extended in a first-author manuscript to source-resolved large-scale functional connectivity. Thus, the basic brain-dynamics phenomenon is already present and reproducible; the proposed work asks what computations organize it.

Behaviour provides complementary mechanistic constraints. Mean accuracy is 83.7\%, and decisions are about 128 ms faster in Fast than Slow blocks (approximately 1186 vs. 1314 ms), confirming robust adaptation to temporal context. Psychophysical kernels---the time-resolved influence of each evidence sample on choice---show strong early weighting, with greater weighting of several early samples under Fast timing. I have compared four sequential-sampling mechanisms using out-of-sample predictive likelihood; among the models tested, a multiplicative urgency account is currently preferred in 28/32 participants. Model-recovery simulations correctly identify the generating model in 47/48 cases, showing that the candidate accounts are distinguishable in the fitted parameter range. I treat the behavioural winner as a quantitative constraint rather than proof of a neural mechanism: the competing models will generate trial-wise predictions that can be adjudicated in neural data. Critically, approximately 10.5\% of trials are initially misleading, so later evidence opposes the emerging choice. On these trials, accuracy improves with deliberation time as later evidence corrects the initial tendency, providing a measurable within-trial test of neural updating and change of mind.

In parallel, I am actively analysing an independent cohort of 74 healthy adults performing a dynamic face-morph task with 256-channel EEG and individual MRI. Existing analyses in this cohort identify evidence-, decision-variable-, and urgency-related EEG signals \citep{9}. My preliminary behavioural reanalysis identifies 42 early and 32 late responders during ambiguous trials, and current analyses are testing whether these decision strategies are expressed in low-dimensional EEG dynamics and large-scale coordination. The MEG dataset therefore provides detailed within-trial mechanistic tests, while the larger EEG cohort provides an independent window on generalization and individual differences, including exploratory links to stable behavioural or personality traits where such measures are available.

\nsercsection{Central hypothesis and objectives}
I hypothesize that dynamic decisions emerge from evolving brain-wide neural states jointly shaped by current sensory evidence, recent evidence history, and the urgency to act as time passes. Controlled changes and reversals in evidence will reveal how strongly prior information constrains these states, while late contradictory evidence will determine whether commitment becomes effectively resistant to new information or remains revisable.

\textbf{Aim 1 --- Determine how evidence, urgency, and distributed coordination organize brain-wide decision dynamics.} Building on the replicated oscillatory trajectories, I will quantify how momentary evidence, temporally weighted evidence history, elapsed time/urgency, and proximity to commitment explain the evolving source-resolved neural state. Rather than asking only where these variables are represented, I will test whether they occupy distinguishable dimensions of the distributed state space and how their relative contributions change as a decision forms. Source-level connectivity will determine how large-scale coordination evolves across deliberation and commitment. I predict that evidence-related and urgency-related information will explain partially separable components of the neural state, accompanied by systematic changes in inter-regional coordination as commitment approaches. A small set of complementary complexity measures will be used only where informative to characterize broader changes in global brain state.

\textbf{Aim 2 --- Determine how brain-wide neural states update when evidence changes or reverses.} Misleading trials provide a direct perturbation of the evolving decision. I will estimate neural evidence-history kernels: at each moment, the influence of evidence received at successive earlier moments on the current neural state. I will combine them with trajectory geometry to quantify the direction and speed with which the state is redirected after evidence changes sign, and compare Fast and Slow blocks to test whether temporal weighting adapts to the cost of waiting. The key alternatives are mechanistically distinct: strong persistence predicts prolonged influence of earlier evidence and slower redirection after reversal, whereas stronger discounting predicts shorter history dependence and faster updating. I predict that the influence of past evidence will decay with lag and that its effective timescale will shorten when commitment pressure is greater, consistent with adaptive integration in dynamic environments \citep{10,13}.

\textbf{Aim 3 --- Determine whether commitment corresponds to a transition in brain-wide dynamics and whether that state remains reversible.} Recent invasive work suggests that commitment may coincide with a rapid change from input-driven to more internally sustained population dynamics \citep{8}, while human behaviour shows that decision termination can flexibly adjust with task demands \citep{14}. I will test whether an analogous transition is detectable at the whole-brain level in humans using convergent state-space and change-point analyses. Candidate transition times will be validated behaviourally by asking whether evidence arriving after the transition has less influence on choice than evidence arriving before it. Dynamic evidence then enables the critical test: on late misleading trials, does contradictory evidence fail to alter an already committed state, or can it redirect the neural trajectory and predict a change of mind? Either outcome constrains the mechanism without assuming in advance that commitment must be irreversible.

\nsercsection{Approach, generalization, and individual differences}
MRI-informed source reconstruction will provide cortical time series for oscillatory state-space, connectivity, and encoding analyses. Trial-wise latent quantities from competing decision models will be related to neural dynamics using cross-validation, matched surrogate controls, and across-participant stability tests. The claims concern distributed brain-wide dynamics, acknowledging the spatial limits and leakage structure of MEG source estimation; connectivity will therefore be interpreted as large-scale functional coordination rather than direct anatomical coupling.

The EEG cohort will test whether the broader organization identified in MEG generalizes across task, recording modality, and individuals. I will test whether early and late responders differ in the neural balance between evidence and urgency, in the timing of commitment-related state changes, and in large-scale coordination. Because urgency has been proposed to vary systematically across individuals \citep{9,15}, independently measured behavioural or personality traits, where available, will provide an exploratory test of whether stable characteristics relate to neural-computational decision profiles. Group labels will remain descriptive summaries of underlying continuous variation rather than assumed biological categories.

\nsercsection{Significance and feasibility}
This project will move human dynamic-decision neuroscience from identifying decision-related signals to explaining how distributed neural states evolve, coordinate, update, and commit while evidence itself changes. It will provide mechanistic tests of evidence-history dependence, adaptive updating, network reconfiguration, and commitment reversibility at a brain-wide scale, while determining which principles generalize across two dynamic-decision paradigms and across individuals. Feasibility is high because both datasets are already collected and under active analysis; the core MEG trajectory results are replicated in my ongoing work; behavioural model comparison and recovery, psychophysical kernels, and reversal-relevant trial definitions are established; and source-level analyses are operational. The resulting first-author MEG replication and connectivity extension will be submitted as part of this doctoral work, with analysis code released openly.
